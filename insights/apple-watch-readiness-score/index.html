Apple Watch Readiness score: what 0–10 means, and why it shows no data

Readiness turns activity, vitals and sleep into one daily number. It is useful when you read it against your own baseline — and easy to misread when you do not.

By Albert Arnó · 7 November 2026 · 7 min read

EN CA ES

In brief: Readiness is a 0–10 score that Apple introduced in September 2026 with Apple Watch Series 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4. It combines recent activity and Training Load, daytime and overnight vitals compared with your personal baseline, and last night’s sleep score, and labels the result Recover (0–1), Pace Yourself (2–4), Ready (5–7) or Go For It (8–10). It only works on those two models, for people aged 13 or older, and needs about a week of overnight data before it shows a score. Apple describes it as a general wellness feature, not a medical measurement.

Most wearables already tell you how much you moved, how you slept and what your heart did overnight. The hard part has always been combining those signals into a decision: train hard today, keep it easy, or rest. Readiness is Apple’s answer — one number each morning, with an explanation of what moved it.

Because it is new, the questions we see most are practical rather than scientific: why my watch does not have it, why it says “no data”, and why it fell after lunch. This guide answers those first, then explains how to read the score well.

What goes into the score

Activity

Active calories, workout effort ratings and Training Load from today and recent days. In Apple’s words, more activity in general can lower the score, while steady or reduced activity raises it.

Vitals

Overnight heart rate, respiratory rate, wrist temperature, heart rate variability (HRV) and blood oxygen, plus daytime heart rate — each compared with your own baseline. Metrics flagged as off lower the score; typical or favourable ones raise it. Higher-than-usual HRV pushes it up.

Sleep

Last night’s sleep score and your recent sleep history. Short, poor-quality or irregular sleep lowers Readiness.

Updates during the day

The score is not fixed in the morning. It changes with calories burned, naps and daytime heart rate, and the app shows which factor moved it.

What each range means

Apple groups the 0–10 score into four labels. The interpretation column is ours: a practical reading, not Apple’s wording.

ScoreLabelA practical reading
8–10Go For ItActivity, vitals and sleep all look at or better than your usual. A reasonable day for your hardest planned session.
5–7ReadyNormal capacity. Train as planned; nothing in the data argues for changing course.
2–4Pace YourselfSomething is pulling the score down — recent load, short sleep or vitals off baseline. Keep intensity moderate and check which factor the app highlights.
0–1RecoverSeveral signals point the same way. Prefer rest or very easy movement, and look for an explanation: a hard block of training, illness, alcohol, travel or poor sleep.

Which Apple Watch models have Readiness?

Only Apple Watch Series 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4, for users aged 13 or older. The feature relies on the new Health Sensing System in those watches, which measures heart rate every five seconds and HRV as often as every five minutes, and adds a daytime vitals view.

watchOS 27 itself runs on Apple Watch Series 9 and later, but installing it on an older watch does not add the Readiness app. If you have a Series 11, Ultra 3, SE 3 or earlier, you will not see a score — although most of the ingredients (sleep score, Vitals, Training Load) are still available to read individually.

Why your Readiness score shows “no data”

On a supported watch, a missing score almost always means one of Apple’s setup requirements is not met. Check them in this order:

  1. Health details. Height, weight and age must be entered in Health Details (Fitness app on iPhone).
  2. Activity. Activity must be set up on the watch.
  3. Overnight vitals and seven nights. Set up overnight vitals and sleep with the watch for at least seven nights. Vitals are compared with a baseline that needs at least 7 of the past 49 nights, so a new watch or a long break resets the wait.
  4. Sleep, last night. Sleep must be set up and enabled, and you need to have slept with the watch the previous night. A night on the charger is the most common reason for a blank morning.
  5. Where to look. Open the Readiness app on the watch, or on iPhone open Fitness and tap Readiness (it may be near the bottom).

Why the score drops during the day

A morning Ready that becomes Pace Yourself after a long ride is the feature working as designed. Readiness updates with calories burned and daytime heart rate, so hard activity lowers it in real time, and a nap or a quiet afternoon can nudge it back. Read the morning value to plan the day and the evening value as a summary of what the day cost.

How to read it well

  1. Compare with yourself, not with others. The vitals component is built on your personal baseline. A 6 for one person and a 6 for another are not comparable, and neither is a fitness ranking.
  2. Expect low scores after hard training. Training stress is supposed to lower readiness for a day or two. A low score after a deliberate hard session is information, not a warning.
  3. Act on patterns, not single days. Several days of Pace Yourself or Recover without a training explanation are more meaningful than one low morning. Check sleep, illness, alcohol, travel and stress before anything else.
  4. Let symptoms override the number. Apple notes that Readiness does not account for existing conditions or medications and should not be the only basis for deciding whether exercise is safe. Chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness or palpitations need medical attention whatever the score says.

What we know — and do not know — about its accuracy

Apple says the Readiness algorithm was developed using data from the Apple Heart and Movement Study, and that the new sensors improve heart rate accuracy. What has not yet been published is an independent validation of the score itself: whether a low Readiness predicts worse performance, injury or illness better than simpler measures such as resting heart rate, HRV trend and sleep duration.

That is normal for a feature that is weeks old, and it shapes how much weight the number deserves. The underlying signals — HRV relative to baseline, resting heart rate, sleep and training load — have a reasonable research base for monitoring recovery. The composite score is a convenient summary of them, not a new physiological measurement. Apple classifies Readiness as a general wellness feature.

If your watch does not have Readiness

You can follow the same logic by hand: look at your HRV and resting heart rate against your usual range, your sleep over the last few nights and your recent training load, and ask whether they agree. When two or three point the same way, the conclusion is about as strong as a composite score would give you. Frequently asked questions

Which Apple Watch has the Readiness score?
Apple Watch Series 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4, for users aged 13 or older. Updating an older watch to watchOS 27 does not add it.
Why does my Readiness score say no data?
Usually a setup gap: Health details missing, Activity or Sleep not set up, fewer than seven nights of overnight vitals, or the watch was not worn to sleep last night.
What is a good Readiness score?
5–7 (Ready) means normal capacity and 8–10 (Go For It) means your signals look better than usual. The score is relative to your own baseline, so a lower number after hard training is expected.
Why did my Readiness score go down during the day?
It updates with calories burned, naps and daytime heart rate, so a workout or a busy day lowers it in real time.
Is the Readiness score a medical measurement?
No. Apple describes it as a general wellness feature that should not replace professional medical judgment or be the only basis for deciding whether it is safe to exercise.
